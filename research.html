Mathematics of Transformers

What would a transformer look like if you derived it from first principles?

A language model predicts the next token from the ones before it. We model that setting with a hidden Markov model, in which the observed tokens are generated by a hidden process, and treat next-token prediction as a nonlinear filtering problem. Recasting the prediction as an optimal control problem yields the dual filter, an inference algorithm whose layer structure closely parallels a decoder-only transformer.

The same viewpoint asks what a transformer computes when it reads a signal from a dynamical system, and leads to filter-based networks that learn hidden Markov models from data.

A newer strand carries these ideas into reinforcement learning. An agent that must act on a partially observed system faces the same problem as a language model: infer a hidden state from a sequence of observations, then decide. We use the filtering and optimal-control view of transformer-like architectures to design and analyze RL agents that act on the beliefs they compute.

Nonlinear Filtering

How do you estimate what you cannot see, when nothing is linear or Gaussian?

Nonlinear filtering computes the conditional probability of a hidden state given noisy observations. The project has two strands that feed each other: a theory of duality between filtering and control, and algorithms built from interacting particles.

Theory

Duality, observability and filter stability

Led by Jin Won Kim

Kalman showed that, for linear systems, estimation and control are duals of each other. Extending that duality to hidden Markov models had been an open problem for six decades. Our extension expresses stochastic observability as controllability of a dual control system, and the filtering problem itself as a dual optimal control problem, an exact extension of the Kalman–Bucy duality. The theory is used to study filter stability: when a filter forgets a wrong initial guess.

This work earned Jin Won Kim the Best Student Paper Award at IEEE CDC 2019 and the CSL Ph.D. Thesis Award. The “Arrow of time” article below is an accessible introduction.

Algorithms

Feedback particle filter and controlled interacting particle systems

Led by Anant Joshi

Our best-known contribution is the feedback particle filter (FPF), which generalizes the Kalman filter to nonlinear, non-Gaussian problems. Instead of the importance weights and resampling of a conventional particle filter, each particle is moved by a feedback control law with the same structure as the Kalman filter’s update. The FPF belongs to a broader class of controlled interacting particle systems, related to the ensemble Kalman filter.

The same ideas carry over to control. Anant Joshi’s work uses log transform duality for stochastic control and reinforcement learning: the log transform converts the cost function into a probability density, and an interacting particle system is used to sample from it. The construction is inspired by the success of interacting particle methods such as the ensemble Kalman filter in filtering. The resulting algorithms, including the dual ensemble Kalman filter, are sample efficient compared with benchmark algorithms on high-dimensional problems.

\[ dX^i_t = a(X^i_t)\,dt + \sigma(X^i_t)\,dB^i_t + \mathsf{K}(X^i_t,t)\circ\Big( dZ_t - \tfrac{1}{2}\big(h(X^i_t) + \hat h_t\big)\,dt \Big) \]

The feedback particle filter: particle \(i\) follows the model plus a correction driven by the observation \(Z_t\). The gain \(\mathsf{K}\) solves a Poisson equation.

Neuroscience

What is synchronization for?

Rhythms are everywhere in the brain, and populations of neurons synchronize and desynchronize as they work. Classical models of coupled oscillators, such as Kuramoto’s, explain how a phase transition from incoherence to synchrony happens, but not why it should. We approach the functional role of synchronization through mean-field games and mean-field control, where collective rhythms arise from the decisions of many interacting agents.

Current work studies collective dynamics and cross-frequency coupling with oscillator models and McKean–Vlasov formulations, together with neural algorithms for large interacting systems.

Soft Robotics: the CyberOctopus

How does an octopus control an arm with no joints and almost infinite degrees of freedom?

Octopus arms coordinate nearly infinite degrees of freedom to reach, grasp, fetch, crawl and swim. In the CyberOctopus project, a multidisciplinary university research initiative in the Coordinated Science Laboratory supported by the Office of Naval Research, we built mathematical models of the arm and used them to control it.

The arm is modeled as a Cosserat rod with biomechanically realistic longitudinal, transverse and oblique muscles. Describing the muscles through a stored energy function made energy shaping control practical, avoiding the hardest step of the method (solving the matching conditions), and produced remarkably life-like reaching and grasping in three dimensions.

On the sensorimotor side, neural models of the arm’s nervous system turned local sensory signals into coordinated muscle activity: a sensory feedback control law reproduced the bend propagation seen in real octopus arms. The work drew on biology upstream, with Rhanor Gillette’s experiments on octopus physiology, and fed robotics downstream, on the BR2 soft robot.

Mean Field Games

When do many selfish agents suddenly start moving together?

The objective was to explore phase transition and self-organization in large population dynamic systems, and the classical question of emergent behavior. In dynamical systems, the emergent collective behavior of a large population is regarded as a phase transition.

London’s Millennium Bridge is a simple example that captures all the main features: a collection of heterogeneous nonlinear systems (pedestrians) with selfish goals (crossing the bridge while keeping their balance) that undergoes a phase transition from an incoherent state to a synchronized one, in which the bridge sways violently.

Modeling each agent as an oscillator and its goal through a mean-field game showed that synchronization of coupled oscillators is a game, with a bifurcation from incoherence to synchrony. Applications range from neuroscience and biology to economics and energy-efficient buildings.
